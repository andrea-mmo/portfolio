The brief

Embrava is a training gear brand I created: four warm colours (dark, terracotta, sand and cream) and a range of bottles, bags, apparel and accessories, each made in the same palette. It had an identity but nowhere to sell.

The store had three jobs: feel unmistakably like the brand, make choosing a colour effortless, and encourage people to buy sets rather than single pieces.

The approach

I explored three directions first: a dark editorial look, a warm catalogue and a kit-led store. The final design combines the last two: a calm, colour-first catalogue, with kits leading the homepage to lift the average order.

Along the way I also reworked the logo itself, splitting it into a clean wordmark and a symbol that can live on its own, and carried the change through every product photo and illustration.

Key decisions

  1. Colour first

    Every product shows its colour options as swatches that swap the photo in place. The chosen colour carries through to the product page and into the link, so a shared product opens in the right colour.

  2. Kits as the best deal

    “Where do you train?” leads straight to three kits for the gym, home and beginners, each saving 15–16%. A kit is always slightly cheaper than building the same set yourself, so the bundle stays the obvious choice.

  3. Build your own kit

    Shoppers can pick any three pieces in any colours and unlock 15% off. A progress bar shows how close they are, turning the discount into a small goal.

  4. A free delivery nudge

    Free UK delivery starts at £50, just above a typical single order. The basket shows exactly how much is left, encouraging one more piece.

  5. Launch pricing for a new brand

    Prices sit in the mid-market, below the big sportswear names and clearly above budget gear: premium enough to signal quality, honest enough for a first purchase from an unknown brand.

  6. Ready to grow into a real shop

    Products, prices, kits and copy live in one data file, kept apart from the design, so the catalogue can move to Shopify when the brand is ready to sell.

Feature spotlight

Shopping on the phone

People find brands like this through social media, so the phone experience came first. Layouts stack into one column, buttons grow to thumb size, and the key paths to a sale stay short.

  • 1. Kits lead the way
  • 2. A thumbnail per colour
  • 3. Build your own kit

  • A sticky add button

    On product pages, a compact bar with the product and an “Add” button follows the shopper once the main button scrolls out of view.

  • A basket that remembers

    The basket slides in from the side, keeps its contents between pages and visits, and shows the free delivery tracker at the top.

  • Accessible by default

    Swatches behave as radio buttons for screen readers, focus is always visible, and animations respect reduced-motion settings.

Product pages

Pages that sell the set, not just the piece

Each product page answers questions in the order shoppers ask them: what is it, how much, which colour, then why buy more.

  • The kit offer appears on its own

    If a product belongs to a kit, the page shows the saving and lets the shopper add the whole kit in one tap.

  • Complete the look

    Suggestions come from the same kit first, then the rest of the range, shown in the colour the shopper already chose.

  • Honest product details

    Details only describe what the photos show. Materials and sizes are left out until they are confirmed with a supplier.

Brand evolution

From a wave-shaped m to sun and sea

The first logo drew the m as a wave inside the word. It had personality, but at small sizes, stitched or printed, it was hard to read and could look like a typo. I kept the name and the palette and split the logo into two pieces that work together or apart.

  • A clean wordmark

    Spaced capitals in Jost Light, with no tricks, so the name reads at any size. Jost also became the typeface for the whole store.

  • A symbol of its own

    A circle cut by a wave: sun and sea. It no longer has to look like a letter, so it can sit alone on caps, socks, labels and social media.

  • Every product updated

    I replaced the old logo on all the product photos and redrew the kit illustrations, choosing the full lockup or the symbol depending on the space on each piece.
