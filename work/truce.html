Truce

A skincare routine organiser for people whose skin keeps breaking out. It builds the routine from the products they already own and helps them find out which one is the problem, one product at a time.

The problem

Many people with breakouts keep adding products: a salicylic cleanser, an acid toner, retinol, two vitamin C serums. When the skin gets worse, there is no way to tell which product is to blame, because everything changed at once.

Most skincare apps answer by recommending more products. I wanted the opposite: an app that takes things away, and tells you when it is time to see a professional.

The approach

I built the app around the method a dermatologist would suggest. First a reset: six weeks with a simple base routine so the skin calms down. Then reintroduction, one product every two weeks from the lowest to the highest risk, with weekly check-ins to decide whether each product stays.

Everything else, the daily routine, the ingredient reading and the advice, exists to make that method easy to follow on a phone.

Key decisions

  1. A rule engine decides, not AI

    The same products always give the same result. The engine is plain code with no screen attached, it returns a rule id and the data, and the words come from translation files. AI is used for one thing only: reading a label from a photo, and the person confirms every ingredient line by line.

  2. Every rule has a source

    179 ingredients are checked against the European Commission's CosIng database, each one stored with its source. The rules are written down in plain language, with an evidence level, and marked as pending professional review. The app says so too.

  3. Safety comes before the routine

    A short check (deep painful bumps, marks that stay, pregnancy, prescription medication, signs of infection) shows a “worth having a professional look at it” alert above everything else. It cannot be dismissed by a good routine.

  4. Language is part of the spec

    “An ingredient to watch”, never “comedogenic, avoid it”. “Worth having a professional look at it”, never a diagnosis. Safety texts are fixed templates, never generated, and the same rules apply in English and Spanish.

  5. The routine comes from real products

    The person adds what they own. Truce builds the morning and evening routine from it, spaces night treatments safely across the week (azelaic acid, salicylic acid, retinol), and flags the classic mistakes: acids without sunscreen, too much exfoliation, the same active in three products, benzoyl peroxide with retinol.

  6. Adding a product without typing a label

    Ingredient lists can be pasted, scanned on the device with OCR, found by barcode in the open Open Beauty Facts database, or read from a photo. Whatever the route, the person confirms the list before it is used.

  7. Health data stays on the device

    Photos of the skin, the diary and the check-ins never leave the phone by default. Optional sync between devices is end-to-end encrypted: the key comes from a passphrase that never leaves the device, and the server only ever stores scrambled text.

  8. Rewarding consistency, not buying

    A gentle points system rewards finishing the routine and doing the weekly check-in. Nothing is awarded for owning more products, which would work against the whole idea.

Behind the scenes

Details that make it trustworthy

  • An invented case that doubles as a test

    Ada uses 16 products and no sunscreen. The document describing what the engine must say about her is also an automated test, so a change that alters the advice is caught straight away.

  • 300+ tests

    The engine, the data, the sync and the translations are all tested. One test checks that English and Spanish have exactly the same texts and nothing is left untranslated.

  • Works offline, installs like an app

    It is a web app that can be added to the phone's home screen and keeps working without a connection, with backup and restore of everything as a file.

  • Keys that never reach the browser

    The label-reading AI sits behind a server function, so the API key is never exposed, and the private version is open only to its owner.
